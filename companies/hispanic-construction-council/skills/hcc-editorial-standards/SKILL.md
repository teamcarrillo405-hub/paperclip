# HCC Editorial Standards

HCC's entire growth strategy depends on being trusted. A single fabricated or
careless statistic, published under a national nonprofit's name, does more damage
than a year of good content repairs. These rules are not negotiable.

## The sourcing rule

**Every factual claim carries a real, checkable source.**

If you cannot point to where a number came from, it does not get published. Not
softened, not hedged — cut.

## Source hierarchy

1. **Primary government data** — Bureau of Labor Statistics, Census Bureau,
   OSHA, Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries, state labor and licensing
   agencies, Federal Reserve
2. **HCC's own research** — reports HCC has published and stands behind
3. **Peer-reviewed academic work**
4. **Established industry sources** — AGC, NAHB, Dodge, trade press with a named
   author and cited methodology
5. **News reporting** from outlets with editorial standards, for events rather
   than statistics

Below that line — blog posts, vendor marketing, aggregator sites, social media
claims, AI-generated summaries — is not a source. It may point you toward one.
Follow it to the original and cite that.

## Dating every figure

A statistic without a date is not usable. Always:

- Name the period the data covers
- Name the source
- Note when the data was published if it lags the period it describes

"Hispanic workers make up 34% of the construction workforce" → incomplete.
"Hispanic or Latino workers made up 34.0% of the construction workforce in 2024
(BLS Labor Force Statistics, published January 2025)" → usable.

## Before publishing anything, verify

- [ ] Every number traces to a source you actually retrieved, not one you
      remember or assume exists
- [ ] Every source link resolves and says what you claim it says
- [ ] Every figure is dated
- [ ] Quotes are verbatim and correctly attributed
- [ ] No statistic has drifted from its source's actual scope (national data not
      presented as regional, a subset not presented as the whole)
- [ ] Anything you could not verify has been cut, not softened

## Handling uncertainty

Naming what is not known builds credibility. Hiding it destroys credibility the
moment someone checks.

- When data is thin: "Federal data does not break this out by ownership, so the
  best available estimate comes from…"
- When sources conflict: give both and say which is more reliable and why
- When something is a projection: label it a projection and name whose

## Never

- **Invent a statistic, study, quote, expert, or finding.** Including
  "illustrative" or "placeholder" numbers that might get published.
- Present an estimate as a measurement
- Cite a source you have not actually read
- Use a real person's name in a fabricated quote or scenario
- Present AI-generated imagery as documentary photography of real people, events,
  or jobsites
- Republish another organization's research as HCC's own

## Corrections

When something published turns out to be wrong, correct it visibly and promptly:
fix the piece, note what changed, and tell the Executive Director. A visible
correction costs far less credibility than a quiet edit that someone notices.

## Sensitive territory

Route to the Executive Director, who routes to the President, before publishing:

- Any policy position HCC has not already taken publicly
- Anything naming a specific member, partner, company, or individual
- Immigration and enforcement topics — real to this audience, and easy to get
  wrong in ways that hurt the people HCC serves
- Anything about a named competitor organization
- Legal, tax, or immigration guidance that could be read as professional advice
